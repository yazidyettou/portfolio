Synopsis

A summer that cracks open

In a border village south of Tébessa, frozen in dust and heat, fifteen-year-old Younès suffocates in a shapeless summer routine. His grandmother, a silent seamstress, sets the rhythm of the house with the clatter of her machine. His father, a construction worker on a highway site, embodies a dry, distant authority.

One day, Younès finds a phone with no SIM card. The screen still works : it becomes his refuge. He secretly films the dust, the insects, the smallest details of his world. But his camera also catches what shouldn't be seen — a smuggling handoff at the edge of the village, and soon the forbidden image of his own father, implicated in the trafficking.

As his maternal grandfather's house is torn down by the construction site — taking with it the last traces of his late mother — Younès's world begins to fracture. His grandmother's sudden death brings his older sister Amel, a student in Algiers, back home. She is the only one he dares show his footage to.

Director's Statement

On Younès

Younès continues what has run through my work for several years : transmission, moral responsibility, the weight of what goes unsaid, and the way a border territory shapes those who inhabit it.

The revelation never takes the form of a confrontation. It settles into silences, glances, withheld gestures. The teenager observes, films and archives the world without always measuring the consequences — until he faces an intimate, irreversible choice.

The film adopts a stripped-down, naturalistic narration at the border of documentary and fiction : real locations, unhurried time, non-professional actors.
